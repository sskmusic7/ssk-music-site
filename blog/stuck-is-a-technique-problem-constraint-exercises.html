Stuck is a technique problem: constraint exercises that start the session

Stuck has technical causes: a blank page, a dead loop, an idea you don't believe. Each one has a constraint that gets the session moving again.

  • Craft
  • Session workflow
  • Constraints

The folder on my drive labelled "ideas" passed four hundred sessions a while back. Almost every one of them is eight bars long. Producers talk about being stuck like it's weather, something that rolls in and has to be waited out. Sit with any single one of those sessions and the stuck turns out to have a technical cause: a direction that never got picked, a loop with nowhere to go, a topline the drums keep arguing with. None of those get fixed by waiting. All of them get fixed by a constraint small enough to apply inside the first ten minutes.

Diagnose it before you reach for anything

Stuck reads as one feeling from the outside. From the chair it splits into different working problems, and they need different tools. The blank session is the one where nothing exists yet, and the paralysis is choice: every sample pack, every key and every tempo is still available, and available means undecided. The dead loop is the opposite problem, a good eight bars that refuses to become a song because it answered "what is this?" and never answered "what happens next?". The dishonest loop is rarer and worse. The idea was weak on day one, some part of you clocked it, and no amount of finishing work can start on an idea you don't believe.

Naming which one you have is most of the fix. Applying the blank-page remedy to a dead loop wastes a night, and the dead-loop remedy applied to a blank page is just procrastination with a tempo.

If the page is blank, let the sample decide

The blank session dies from freedom, so remove most of it. Pick one sample before the DAW is even open, and set a rule that the first twenty minutes happen at one tempo with one drum sound. No browsing, none. Scrolling a sample library feels like work and produces nothing; it's the studio version of tidying the kitchen instead of cooking. A chopped vocal, a guitar loop, a single shaker. Twenty minutes later something exists, and something existing is a completely different problem from nothing existing.

Most of the Afroswing era ran this way. The records that defined London from 2016 to 2018 grew from riffs small enough to hold in your head, and the ones I produced in that run, Been Wavey among them, all started as one loop and a tempo. Nothing about a first loop has to be clever. It has to be hummable and short, because a riff you can't remember is a riff the artist can't write to.

If the loop is dead, let the groove decide

A dead loop has an idea but no direction, and in this genre direction is a drum decision before it's a melodic one. Open the drum pattern cheat sheet on the Fun Lab and pick a groove you haven't used this month. If you write amapiano, take the dembow: eight bars of drums only, no melody, no bass, an hour on the clock. If you live in straight fours, take the 3+3+2 and find out what it asks of the bassline. The constraint works because it answers the question the loop was actually asking. A log-drum loop with a dembow pattern over the top is already a different record that happens to share a key, which is exactly the point. You were never short of ideas in that session. You were short of a decision, and a groove is the cheapest decision going.

If you don't believe the idea, let the arrangement decide

The dishonest loop can't be constrained into quality, but it can be tested cheaply. Borrow the section map off a record you trust. Count the bars of intro, verse and hook on something like Face to Face, write the numbers down, then stretch your loop across that map with placeholder copies. No new writing at this stage, pure duplication, ten minutes. What you learn is whether the loop carries a full verse's weight. Strong ideas get more interesting with repetition, which is the whole mechanism behind a tag or a riff that turns into a signature. Weak ideas fall apart by the second eight, and now you know at bar sixteen instead of month three.

The first hour runs on constraints

One distinction to keep straight: everything above is about starting, and starting is a separate skill from finishing. The discipline that carries a session from eight bars to a bounced file has its own failure modes and deserves its own piece. What the first hour decides is simpler. A folder of four hundred dead sessions and a catalogue of released records are made of the same raw material: loops, hours, decisions. The catalogue is what accumulates when the first hour keeps ending with a decision in it.

So set the constraint before you feel ready. One sample, one groove, one borrowed map, one clock. The mood everyone calls inspiration tends to arrive about twenty minutes in, once there's something on the screen worth having a feeling about. It makes a regular guest but a terrible foreman, so don't hand it the session. Decide small, decide early, and let the record earn the right to be finished some other evening.
