The One Drum Hit That Defines Afroswing

Move the snare one beat and a UK rap record becomes an Afroswing record. Here's the displacement that built a genre, and how to program it.

  • Afroswing
  • Production
  • Drum Programming

Most people can hear Afroswing instantly. Most people can't tell you why. It isn't the melody. It isn't the tempo. It's one drum hit, in one place, and moving it back to where hip-hop puts it collapses the whole thing.

The snare lands on the third.

Hip-hop puts it on two and four. Afroswing doesn't.

A hip-hop or trap bar puts its backbeat on two and four. It's the most familiar rhythm in Western popular music, so familiar that producers reach for it without deciding to.

Afroswing doesn't do that. The accent sits on beat three. That single displacement is what the producers who built the sound point to first. Steel Banglez, who produced a good portion of the genre's defining records, has described it exactly this way: the snare comes on the third, where in hip-hop it comes on the fourth, and that placement comes from Afrobeats.

It's inherited, not invented. The beat-three accent runs through Jamaican one-drop, where the kick and rim hit together on three and beat one is deliberately left empty. It runs through the Afrobeats and dancehall records that London was raised on. Afroswing carried that accent into UK rap rather than creating it from nothing.

Why it changes the feel

A two-and-four backbeat pushes. It's a march. It tells you where the bar is at every step, which is exactly what you want behind a rapper who's driving hard.

Moving that accent to three does something different. It leaves beats two and four open. The bar stops insisting on itself, and that space is where the vocal sits. That's why Afroswing vocals sound like they're floating over the beat instead of fighting it. The melodic, half-sung delivery the genre is known for exists because the drums got out of the way.

It also frees up the kick. With the backbeat off two and four, the kick can play dotted pairs: landing on one, again a dotted-eighth later, then repeating from three. That's dancehall's figure, and it locks against the beat-three snare instead of competing with it.

Programming it

On a sixteen-step grid, counting steps 1 to 16:

  • Snare on step 9, and only step 9. Resist adding step 5 or 13. The empty space is the point.
  • Kick on steps 1, 4, 9, 12: one and its anticipation, then the same pair from beat three.
  • Hats in straight eighths with a few sixteenth flicks. The flicks are grime and garage, not Afrobeats. That's where the UK half of the genre shows up.
  • Percussion answering the kick rather than doubling it. A shaker or rim aimed at the gaps.

Swing it lightly, somewhere around 56 to 58 percent. Straight sixteenths sound stiff. Full triplet swing turns it into something else entirely.

Tempo sits near 100 to 105 BPM. Slower than people expect. That's the other thing producers get wrong on a first attempt.

Hear it, then break it

Reading a grid gets you partway there. The drum pattern cheat sheet in the Fun Lab has this pattern loaded and playable, alongside Afrobeats, dancehall, amapiano and the rest. Load it up and you can hear the beat-three snare next to a two-and-four one, and catch the difference inside a couple of bars.

Load it into the beat machine and move the snare to step 5. You'll hear a UK rap beat. Move it back to 9 and you'll hear an Afroswing record.

One hit, one beat over. That's the whole trick.
