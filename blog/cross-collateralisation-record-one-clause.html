Cross-collateralisation and the missing Record One clause

The streams did the numbers and the royalty ledger still reads zero, because the producer points wait behind every cost the deal can reach. One clause decides which paper you signed.

  • Contracts
  • Royalties
  • Music Business

A lease ends clean. The buyer pays, the files move, the terms sit on the receipt, and nothing is owed in either direction afterwards. The catalogue is three hundred and sixty releases deep at the last count, and most of it moves on exactly that paper: fee up front, terms known before anything ships. The habit it builds is a good one. The fee is the money. So when a label conversation finally turns into a deal and the word "royalty" enters the room, most producers hear it as the opposite of a lease. Backend money at last, real participation, a number that grows while the streams do. The default paper says otherwise, and the difference comes down to one clause most deal memos quietly leave out.

Where the points actually sit

Start with where a producer royalty lives on label paper. It is rarely a separate tap running alongside the artist's money. It is carved out of the artist's all-in royalty, and the account it sits in carries every cost the contract allows: recording costs for the whole project, the video and whatever marketing the label chose to spend. Until that account recoups, the producer's points are a figure on a statement rather than a payment.

Cross-collateralisation sits on top of that. It pools recoupment: advances across every record in the deal recoup from royalties earned by any of them. One balance, and everything the label paid drinks from the same pot. Under a 360 the pool reaches further again, into touring and merchandise, but the record-level version is enough to make the point.

The same record on two sets of paper

Say the deal covers three sessions on an album. Eight thousand per advance, twenty-four total, three points on the records produced. One of the three does numbers and the other two die. Round figures, but the shape is standard.

Under the default paper the ledger reads like this. Producer royalty earned on the hit: twelve thousand. Producer advances on the books: twenty-four, all pooled by cross-collateralisation. Recording costs across the album, charged against the same account the producer's points sit in: fifty-five thousand. The account was underwater before the producer's royalty could pay a penny. Payable: zero. On a record that earned a hundred thousand and counting.

Add the Record One language and the same record accounts differently. Record One means the producer's royalty accrues from the first record sold, because recording costs are charged against the artist's royalties and stay off the producer's ledger entirely. The producer's own advances still recoup, from producer royalties, but each advance recoups only against the record it was paid for. Twelve thousand earned on the hit, eight recouped from that session's advance, four payable at the first accounting period. Same streams. Same points. The clause is the only thing that changed.

What Record One means, exactly

The phrase gets misread constantly, so say the mechanic plainly. Customary producer paper pays from record one: the producer participates from the first sale, without waiting for the label's recording costs to recoup. Those costs are always charged against the artist's royalty account, and without Record One treatment the producer's points wait behind them. That wait is the zero. A producer can watch a record do millions of streams while their royalty ledger sits at nought, because the account holding their points is still paying off decisions the label made about studios, features and videos.

The two sentences to ask for

The fix is boring, which is why it works. Two sentences of contract language decide the whole outcome.

First: producer royalties accrue from Record One, and recording costs are not chargeable against producer royalties. That is the customary ask, and naming it as such matters, because business affairs recognises language it has seen before.

Second: advances paid in respect of one record are recoupable only from royalties attributable to that record. That sentence kills the pooling. It matters more than the first one, because Record One on its own can still be swallowed. A royalty that accrues from the first sale can still be recouped away by the advances from the two dead sessions, since cross-collateralisation is the default and carve-outs exist only where the paper puts them.

A first-time producer may not win both outright. If the pooling survives, price it. Take the bigger advance, because the advance is the only money that arrives without a statement, and the statement calendar is where the pool does its waiting.

What the lease instinct already knows

The standard criticism of leases is that they carry no backend. True, and the flat fee is the feature. A fee is known before the files move, and nothing the label spends later can reach back into it. A backend is a number on a statement you cannot see, carrying costs you did not choose, against a pool the contract built in advance. Producers who learned the business on clean paper tend to read recoupment clauses properly, because they already know what a finished transaction feels like. The catalogue runs on that instinct, the same one behind clearing samples before release: paperwork on day one is what stops a zero later. Every deal conversation from here runs on two questions. What is owed now? And what can reach it afterwards? The first question gets the advance. The second one gets the clause.
