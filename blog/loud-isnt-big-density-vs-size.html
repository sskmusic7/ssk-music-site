Loud isn't big: density vs size in a mix

Two records can tie on the meter and still differ in size. Perceived loudness gets bought with contrast and density, size gets bought with space, and the order you buy them in decides what survives the master.

  • Mixing
  • Arrangement
  • Loudness

Pushing the limiter is the most common answer to a record that sounds small, and it is the wrong dial. Loudness and size are separate reads, solved by different moves. A mix can peak hard and still feel thin. Another can sit two dB below the reference and dwarf it. Both problems get fixed in the arrangement, before anything on the mix bus loads.

Loudness is what contrast buys

Perceived loudness tracks transient energy against whatever surrounds it. Load the Afroswing pattern in the Fun Lab and look at where the hits are not. Kick on steps 1, 4, 9 and 12, snare alone on 9, clap answering on 15, and between steps 4 and 9 there is a five-step hole only the shaker crosses, on 3, 7, 11 and 15. The hole is why the pattern punches. The snare on 9 lands into open space, so it reads loud at a lower peak than a snare buried in sixteenths ever will.

Fill that hole and every hit gets quieter by the same meter. Density past a certain point flattens contrast, and flattened contrast reads as quieter even when the LUFS number holds still. This is arrangement-level gain, and it is why an over-stuffed loop can lose a level war against a sparse one. The afroswing beats in this catalogue ("Flashbacks", "Heatwave", the 2021 B Young brief "Fever") live or die on the same logic: the pattern is the genre's floor, and the gaps do as much of the loudness work as the hits.

Density is the cheap loudness

The shaker holding all sixteen steps is the clearest case in the Fun Lab data. Both the Amapiano and Afrobeats patterns run one, steps 1 through 16 with no gaps, and it lifts perceived energy without adding a dB to a single transient. The ear reads continuous texture as level. Play a sparse loop straight after a dense one and the sparse loop sounds quieter than it did five minutes ago. The meter has not moved; the reference has.

Contrast between sections runs the same trick at arrangement scale. Drop the shaker and the percussion lane for the last bar before the hook and the hook reads louder, untouched master included. Volume automation gets the credit for moves like this half the time. The mute button did the work.

Size is real estate, not level

Size is how much field the record occupies: spectral spread, stereo width, and decay time. A big record owns more of all three.

Low end first. One element owns the sub. The Amapiano pattern shows the separation working: log drum on steps 4, 7, 11 and 14, open hat skimming 3, 7, 11 and 15, and the two never share a register. The log drum fills the low mids while the hat lives on top. Put two elements in the same octave and whichever is louder wins. The loser's energy turns into masking, and masking reads as small.

Width belongs to the top, not the bottom. Kick and sub stay centred and mono. Hats, shaker and percussion spread out from the centre line, the shaker a touch off to one side. A mix that is wide at the bottom collapses on club systems. A mix that is narrow at the top just reads flat, which is the cheaper failure.

Space is the third leg. Short rooms on the transients keep the punch's edge. Longer tails on pads and textures let the bar decay into the next one. Pre-delay on the vocal reverb keeps the dry signal in front of the wash. A tail that outlives the hit reads as size. A tail that swallows the transient reads as mud, which is a different problem wearing the same clothes.

The sparse case: UK garage

UK garage is the cleanest proof that loud and big are separate dials, because 2-step records are both with a kick that plays twice a bar. The Fun Lab pattern has the kick on steps 1 and 11 at 135 bpm, snare and clap stacked on 5 and 13, swing pushed to 62%. Nothing in that drum arrangement is dense. The size comes from the swing dragging the hats off the grid and from all the space left where a four-to-the-floor kick would sit. The loudness comes from how hard the few hits that remain can hit without anything competing.

If loudness were only about level, those records would be quiet. They are not.

Order of operations

Size first, loudness last. Build the stereo field and the spectral spread at mix level, then gain-stage the finished mix into the limiter and let it work less. A mix with intact transients needs less limiting to reach the same number, which is the same loudness for less pumping.

The meter and the ear part ways at exactly this point. Two masters at the same integrated LUFS still A/B differently, and the one with more distance between its loud and quiet moments reads louder even though the meters tie. The meter measures energy. The ear measures change.

Why this matters in a beat catalogue

A type beat has to survive a phone speaker in a car park and a car system on the drive home, and the customer has judged it before the second bar. Density and contrast decisions made in the arrangement survive that playback. Limiter gain does not; it turns into distortion exactly on the systems that were already struggling. That is why the pattern pages in the Fun Lab are worth loading before the mix bus is worth touching. The afroswing material holds up outside the room it was made in, and the discography page has the rest of the catalogue to check it against.
