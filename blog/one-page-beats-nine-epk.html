One page beats nine: what an EPK is actually for

An A&R gives the email forty seconds on a phone between sessions. One page with three links survives that; a nine-page PDF does not, and the same rule sells a beat pack.

  • Music Business
  • Sync
  • Producers

Forty seconds is the honest budget for the email you spent a week on. That is roughly what an A&R or a manager gives a pitch before the thumb moves on, and most of those seconds go on finding the play button. The nine-page electronic press kit loses that race before the first bar of audio, because it was built to impress a printer. A one-page kit is built to move a thumb across a phone screen, and it wins the race more often than the PDF ever will.

I have sent a lot of beats out. The Afroswing years ran on links in emails rather than attachments, because the people making decisions were in a car between sessions with one bar of signal. Reading pitches from the other side since, the pattern has held: the kits that got a reply were the ones that finished before I got bored. The catalogue now sits at three hundred and sixty releases, mostly beats sold on lease. If I had to pitch all of it to one person on one phone, I would send one page and a folder. Here is what goes on that page.

The reader is between two sessions

Assume the kit gets opened on a phone, held in one hand, somewhere between a writing camp and the drive home. The reader is not judging your typography. They are triaging. Every screen of scrolling is a fresh chance to close the tab, and a nine-page PDF is nine of those chances in a row. They want to press play. Then they want to check you are who the email says you are. Then they want a button that replies. Anything placed before the audio is a tax on the only thing that matters, and most kits charge that tax for four pages before the first link shows up.

What earns its place on the page

Name and role in one line, next to a photo that looks like you on a normal Tuesday rather than a label budget. Then three records, linked to streaming, chosen for range rather than affection. The catalogue runs from West Coast club bounce pitched at YG and G-Eazy through to Afro-jazz fusion pitched at Ezra Collective and Burna Boy, so the picks have to show that spread: one club record, plus one slow builder that proves the writing holds up without a vocal stapled to it.

Then a credit line, and here is where most kits lie by accident. Do not write streaming numbers you cannot pull up on a dashboard. Do not stretch a placement past what it was. If the sync was a Netflix show, name the show, because the first person who checks a claim you padded tells the second person. A short list of true credits outruns a long list of hopeful ones every time it gets tested.

Last, the terms. If you sell the beat, the page says so in plain sight: fee up front and what the lease includes at each tier. Buyers do not want to email a stranger to discover a number. The pitch that answers the money question inside one screen is the pitch that gets a reply inside one day.

The folder does the work of six pages

The pages after the first are usually biography and flattery, and a folder does both jobs better with nothing but filenames. A file named Trackname 104bpm Fmin prod SSK.wav answers tempo, key and authorship before anybody opens it. Ten of those in a folder, named the same way, is a press kit with the padding stripped out. Nobody in the history of A&R has closed a folder because it was too easy to use.

For sync the folder is not optional at all. Supervisors search by tempo and mood, and they clear rights before they fall in love with anything, so splits and clearance status sit in the metadata where their systems can read them. The pattern breakdowns on the FunLab page explain the writing better than a bio ever could, which is the whole point: show the work, skip the adjectives. A kit that makes a supervisor ask for the BPM has already lost the placement to the kit that answered the question unprompted.

Build the long version anyway, send it second

None of this makes the long kit useless. Press want a bio they can cut and paste. Venues and festivals want photos at print resolution and the complete credit history. Build all of it once, keep it in the same folder tree, and update it the week a placement lands. The pitch email itself stays one page with a link to everything else. Long version on request reads as confidence. Long version attached reads as somebody who has not had an email answered in a while.

The lease taught the habit first

Most of the catalogue moves on the same paper: fee up front, terms known before anything ships. That habit is the entire argument. Decide the terms before the pitch and keep the listen one tap from the thumb. A press kit built the way a lease is built is just a receipt with a photo on it. The range is all here, three hundred and sixty deep, and it pitches exactly the way it ships: one page, one link, terms on the receipt, and nothing standing between the play button and the listener.
