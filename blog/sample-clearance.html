Two copyrights, one sample: what clears and what it costs when you get it wrong

Every sample sits behind two doors: the master and the publishing. What clearing both actually involves, and what an uncleared chop costs once Content ID finds it.

  • Sampling
  • Licensing
  • Business

Every beat in this catalogue went up for sale on the same assumption: whoever buys it is going to put a vocal on it and release it. That assumption makes sampling a business question before it is a creative one. An uncleared sample is the one part of a beat that follows the customer out of the store and lands on their release weeks later, with your production under it and nobody's paperwork behind it.

So here is how clearing actually works, what never clears no matter how it is chopped, and what an uncleared sample costs once a claim lands.

Every sample sits behind two doors

Lift eight bars of someone else's record and you have used two copyrighted works at once. The recording itself, the master, usually belongs to the label that put the record out. The song underneath the recording, the composition, belongs to the writers and is administered by their publisher. Clearing a sample means a yes from both. Each side negotiates separately, and either one can end the conversation on its own.

Co-writes multiply the doors. A track with three writers can have two or three publishers behind it, each with their own idea of a fair deal. The sample is one thing. The permission is never one thing.

What clears

Real clearance starts before the beat goes on sale, not after an artist has written a hook to it.

The master side is a negotiation with the owner of the original recording: an advance up front, often a royalty point on the new record, sometimes both. The publishing side is a share of the new song's composition. The shares commonly quoted run from ten per cent to half the song, and the number moves with how recognisable the sampled part is. A four-bar loop that carries the whole hook costs more than a texture buried under the drums. Nothing about this is standard, which is why it is called a negotiation.

It is also slow. Majors have departments for this and still take weeks. Independent rights holders get answered by email, and answered slowly. Anyone clearing against a release date should assume months.

Replaying the part changes the maths. Re-record the melody yourself and no master was used, so the master negotiation disappears. The composition still needs clearing if it is recognisable, but one negotiation beats two, and a replay that has been re-voiced and re-grooved gives the publisher less of the new record to point at.

What does not clear

The three-second rule is a myth. No sample length is short enough to be free. US courts rejected the idea for sound recordings in Bridgeport v. Dimension Films in 2005, and UK practice has always been conservative: if a listener can hear where it came from, it needs clearing.

Transformation does not launder a sample either. Pitch it up four semitones, reverse it, chop it into stutter rolls. The composition survives all of that, because the notes are still the notes. What actually changes the legal position is changing the notes themselves: a replayed part or a rewritten melody stops being a sample at all.

Sample packs are the genuine exception, and only because the seller cleared everything before selling it. Read the licence even so. Most packs are royalty-free for use in new recordings. A few restrict how the finished track is distributed. A record streamed off Spotify is the opposite of a sample pack: nothing about it is cleared, at any length, in any form.

What it costs when you get it wrong

For a type beat the exposure runs in two directions at once. The claim lands on the customer's release, and it lands on the upload that sold the beat. A catalogue of 289 beats on YouTube is 289 uploads Content ID can match against, and one uncleared chop anywhere in it redirects monetisation on every version it appears in.

The mechanics are unglamorous. Content ID matches the audio, the rights holder files a claim, the revenue flows to them. On DSPs a takedown arrives and the release comes down, and the artist who paid for the beat learns their single cannot stay up. That conversation happens with you rather than with whoever filed the claim, because the licence that came with the beat says, in writing, that sample clearance sits with the buyer. In theory it was their problem. In practice it was your sample.

If a release survives long enough to reach lawyers, the expensive part has already happened: you lost the timing that made clearance affordable. A rights holder approached before release is negotiating a market rate. The same rights holder approached after the record is out can ask for a bigger share, a credit on the sleeve, or the release itself, and get it. Post-release clearance exists. It costs whatever the other side decides it costs.

How this catalogue stays clean

Two working rules, both cheaper than any claim.

Sample your own sessions first. A chop of a beat you made yourself behaves exactly like a sample pack, because you own both sides of it. Resample your own arrangements and flip your own unreleased ideas, and the two-doors problem never opens. Hearing how a finished session stacks, part by part, is also the fastest way to learn to build those parts instead of lifting them. The Stem Replicator in the Fun Lab exists for exactly that.

Rebuild instead of lift. A dancehall kick pattern, an Afrobeats log-drum line, the beat-three snare that defines Afroswing: rhythms are owned by nobody. The sound of this era came from shared rhythms rather than shared audio, and every one of those grooves can be played from scratch on the drum pattern cheat sheet. What you build from them is yours.

And when the actual record genuinely has to be in there, the beat does not go up for sale until the yes exists in writing. That rule has cost me beats I wanted to sell. It has never cost me a takedown.
