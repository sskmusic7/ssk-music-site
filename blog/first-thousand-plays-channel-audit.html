Find the one channel that carried your first 1,000 plays, then stop guessing

Pull traffic sources on the three fastest movers, count the answers, and the channel that carries your traction names itself. A twenty-minute audit, run against 360 releases of catalogue.

  • Marketing
  • Catalogue
  • Analytics

Two records from 2017 hold 27.1 million of the 39.2 million YouTube views across this catalogue. Been Wavey sits at 17.4 million, Erdz Boy at 9.7 million, and both came out the same year. The other 358 releases share the rest, and the median release sits at 2,691 views. That skew is the most useful fact a producer can hold about their own numbers: plays do not spread evenly, and neither does the credit for them. Most producers never find out which channel carried their traction. They keep posting everywhere at an even split, which feels like work and measures like nothing.

The fix is a twenty-minute audit per release. Run it young, while the release still has live traffic to read, and it will name one channel. Then you feed that channel and stop guessing.

Decide what a play is before you count anything

A YouTube view needs thirty seconds of watch time. A Spotify stream needs thirty seconds too. A free download with an email attached is a lead, not a play, and if you fold it into the count the audit is polluted from row one. Write the definition down once, per platform, and never change it mid-audit. You are comparing channels against each other, so the units have to match. Vague counting produces confident wrong answers, which are worse than no answers.

Pull traffic sources per release, not per channel

YouTube Studio keeps the answer behind three clicks. Open the video, open Analytics, open the Reach tab, then read the traffic source breakdown. Do this on individual videos, never on the channel aggregate. The channel view averages away exactly the spike you are hunting for.

Pick the right videos too. Sorting by total views surfaces your oldest winners, which is only half the story. Sort by views against days since upload and you surface what moved fastest, and early traction is the whole subject here. In this catalogue 252 of 360 releases have cleared a thousand views. The audit question is which source cleared it for each one: YouTube search, suggested videos, an external link, or the browse feed.

The catalogue already ran this test

The discography splits cleanly into two channel stories, and the split is stark.

The tracks tell the first one. Been Wavey (B Young, 2017) sits at 17.4 million views. Erdz Boy (Mowgs, 2017) at 9.7 million. Juice (B Young, 2018) at 3.5 million. Extra (Ambush x Sp Montiz, 2017) at 1.9 million. On those records the channel was the artist's audience. A placement borrows an audience that already exists: fans of B Young pressed play on a song they wanted to hear, and the producer credit collected the exposure. That is a channel you cannot post your way into. You earn it by sending beats to the right artists, which is why the afroswing era dominates the top of the catalogue.

The beats tell the second story. The biggest own-channel upload, African Dream, a J Hus x Yxng Bane x Burna Boy afroswing type beat, sits at 196,012 views. Brooklyn Cream follows at 143,995, then the Mist x Mostack afroswing beat at 107,011. Those plays came off YouTube search, and the catalogue's own titles show the mechanism. The naming format reads like a search bar: "(Free) South of the Border - Sabrina Carpenter Type Beat". The title is the query. When search is the channel, the title format is the lever, and it costs nothing to turn.

One channel needs artists. The other needs titles. They demand different weeks and different kinds of patience, which is exactly why the audit has to name one before you spend a month on the wrong one.

The four-step audit

  1. List every release from the last 90 days with its view count and upload date. Ninety days is the window where early traction either shows or never arrives.
  2. Open the traffic source breakdown on the three fastest movers, not the three biggest. Screenshot each one.
  3. Write one word per release: search, suggested, external or browse.
  4. Count the words. The word that wins is your channel.

That is the entire method. It fits on an index card and it works at any catalogue size, because the question never changes: of the plays this release actually got, which door did they walk through?

Double down on what it names

If search wins, the work is titles and tags. The catalogue is the proof: releases named like a search query outperform clever titles across every own-channel upload in the discography. Make three more beats against the same search phrase before you make one against a new idea. Repetition of a winning query compounds. Clever titles die quietly.

If the artist's audience wins, the work is placements. That means packs sent to artists who fit the pocket, not mass emails, and the pocket itself is learnable: the drum pattern cheat sheet on this site breaks down where afroswing puts its snare and why the kick pushes ahead of it.

If the audit comes back with nothing above a trickle on every source, that is an answer too. The release told you the song is the problem, not the marketing. Fold it and make the next one better.

The audit costs twenty minutes. Guessing costs a quarter.

Re-run it every release

At 360 releases this catalogue is a dataset, and the skew inside it is the lesson. Plays concentrate. Two records from one year carry more than two-thirds of 39 million views. Channel effort should concentrate the same way. Run the audit while each release is young, name the door, and make three more of whatever the door asks for.
