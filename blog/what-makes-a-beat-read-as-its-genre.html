Log drums, pushed kicks and one swing setting: what makes a beat read as its genre

A type beat's tag is a promise the first bar has to keep. The exact moves that make a beat read as amapiano, dancehall or UK garage, and which of them survive being played badly.

  • Amapiano
  • Dancehall
  • UK Garage
  • Production

A type beat makes a promise with its tag, and the promise gets checked in the first bar. The customer searched the genre, the upload says the genre, and the opening loop has to agree before the melody has said a word. This catalogue has worn two of these tags. "SwingYano" answered a Pabi Cooper x Burna Boy amapiano brief, and "All Night" and "Baby" were dancehall cuts for Tory Lanez and PartyNextDoor. UK garage has never turned up in a brief here, which is part of why it earns a section below: it teaches the cleanest lesson of the three.

What the tag actually promises turns out to be small. A handful of moves, in the right positions, doing the signifying while everything else decorates. Here are those moves for the three genres the Fun Lab carries patterns for, step numbers included, plus what survives being played badly.

Amapiano: the hat between the kicks

Load the Amapiano pattern and the first thing through is an open hat on the offbeat: steps 3, 7, 11 and 15, the third sixteenth of every beat. That tss landing between the kicks is the sound the genre runs on, and it is a recent development; the same position was being played as a closed hat before producers heard what the open hat did to it. Under it a shaker holds all sixteen steps, the kick walks four-on-the-floor and then pushes the and of 4 for the Pitori bounce, and a rim answers instead of repeating.

Then the move that sells the record: the log drum, sitting on steps 4, 7, 10 and 13, deliberately late of the kick. It is a pitched, wooden bass that slides between notes rather than locking to them, and it usually waits out the intro. When it enters, that entrance is the drop. Nothing else in the arrangement has to change for the record to lift.

Tempo runs 108 to 115 BPM with 112 as the centre. The chords sit wide and jazzy, and they are furniture: pleasant, carefully voiced, but nobody is keying in on them.

So what survives bad execution? The chords, the mix, the keys. A log drum slightly out of tune with everything around it still reads as amapiano, because the signifier is positional, not tonal. Swap it for a clean 808 playing the same line square to the kick and the record stops saying anything at all.

Dancehall: the pushed kick

The Dancehall pattern looks wrong on first read. Kick on 1 and 3 with pushes on the and of 2 and the and of 4: steps 1, 7, 9 and 15. No snare lane at all. A rim answers on the last sixteenth of every beat, the hats walk straight eighths, and an open hat shoves the same two offbeats the kick shoves.

That bounce is one riddim family out of several, and knowing the families is part of the genre's grammar. One-drop empties beat 1 and lands kick and rim together on 3, the accent an earlier post here traced into UK rap. Steppers runs the kick through every beat instead. The pushed bounce is the one most requests mean, and it runs slow: around 90 to 95 BPM, slower than most people program it on a first attempt.

The structural move is older than any of the programming. A riddim is written to be voiced repeatedly: one instrumental, a queue of artists, the top end deliberately open because a deejay owns it. King Jammy's Sleng Teng riddim, built from a Casio preset in 1985, carried that system into the digital era, and the system never left. Programming dancehall means programming a bed rather than a finished record.

Break the bounce and the tag stops being true. Put a beautiful bassline under a kick sitting square on 2 and 4 and you have built a pop record waiting for a patois vocal. Keep the bounce and the riddim rolls even when the hats were programmed with all the feel of a spreadsheet.

UK garage: one number

UK garage has never shown up in a brief here. It gets its section anyway, because its genre-carrying move is a single number, cleaner than any drum placement. Swing, at 55 to 60 percent.

The pattern on the sheet: kick on 1, then nothing until halfway through beat 3, step 11, skipping the beat you expected. Snare dead straight on 2 and 4. Hats landing late against that quantised backbeat, with a shaker filling the gaps the hats leave. The tension between swung everything and a straight snare is the entire feel of two-step.

Flatten the swing to 50 and the beat dies with the best samples money can buy. Push it toward 70 and it limps. The window is narrow and mechanical, which is why a bad attempt at UKG usually fails exactly there and nowhere else. Everything else has slack: a cheap hat still swings, a thin bass still interlocks with that two-step kick. Load the pattern into the beat machine and drag the swing from 50 to 58 inside one bar. You can hear the genre switch on.

What carries the tag

Each of these genres leans on one element hardest: the open hat plus log drum for amapiano, the pushed kick for dancehall, the swing figure for UK garage. Afroswing's beat-three snare, covered here before, is the same story one genre over. That element is what a listener checks against the tag in the first bar, before the melody has finished its first phrase.

Hybrid briefs run on the same logic. Half the amapiano requests in this catalogue are fusions: SwingYano is tagged Amapiano x Afro-Fusion, Wonderful is Afro-R&B x Amapiano. A hybrid works by borrowing the signifier and leaving the rest of the tradition at the door.

The test that follows is worth running on your own beats. Mute the signifier and see what is left. The amapiano pattern without its log lane is house with a shaker habit. The dancehall pattern without its pushes is a straight reggae-pop bed. UKG without swing is nothing in particular.

And the reverse is the commercial point. A rough record with the right moves in the right places still passes the first bar, and the first bar is where the sale happens. Polish reads as polish. Placement reads as genre.
